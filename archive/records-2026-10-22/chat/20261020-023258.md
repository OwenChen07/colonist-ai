Understood, the question is planning over several turns, not a one-move tactic. That is harder, and here is why and what works.

**Why the bot does not do it now.** A three-road join pays off three or four turns away, with dice rolls in between. The search at 2,400 simulations through chance nodes sees one or two of its own turns clearly, so the plan's value must come from the value head. The value head learns from outcomes of self-play games, and self-play almost never executes a three-road plan, because the net does not do it. So the data never shows the payoff, and the net never learns it. That is the same fixed point as the strength plateau, seen on one skill.

**Three ways to teach it, and they stack:**
1. **Plan-summary features.** The engine computes, for each player, the longest road reachable with 1, 2 and 3 more roads, the road distance between their networks, and roads needed to reach the best free spot. A multi-turn plan becomes a number the value head can read in one step. Chess engines do this with mobility and pawn-structure terms; it is the cheapest path and usually the biggest gain.
2. **Targets that reward recognising the plan.** Auxiliary targets for the road race 10 turns ahead and at the end, so the net is graded on seeing the race coming, not only on the final result. We already have end-of-game Longest Road as a weak auxiliary target.
3. **Self-play that tries the plans,** so the payoff enters the data: start self-play games from positions where a 2-3 road join is available, and a rule-based road-racing opponent in the pool so the net gets punished for ignoring the race. This is where the league idea and the rehex rules meet.

What does not help: longer search alone, since dice flatten its horizon, and the usual reinforcement-learning fix of n-step value targets, since our value is already trained on full game outcomes; the gap is data, not credit assignment.
