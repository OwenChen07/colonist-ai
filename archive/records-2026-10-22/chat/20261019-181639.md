No, the gate is not what is holding us back, and aiming at smaller gains would hurt more than help.

**The numbers.** To be confident a net is really better, not just lucky, with the usual 95% confidence and an 80% chance of catching a real gain:

| true gain | games needed |
|---|---|
| 2 points (52%) | about 4,000 |
| 1.5 points | about 7,000 |
| 1 point (51%) | about 16,000 |
| 0.5 point | about 60,000 |

Today's gate already covers 1.5 points with its 8,000-game extension. Going to 1 point means 16,000 games, about 2 to 3 hours of the fleet per round, which doubles the round time and halves the number of rounds. Below that it is not practical per round.

**Why it would not matter anyway.** We already have the certainty you ask about, from pooling: rounds 111 to 121 scored 49.9% over 68,000 games against round 73, with a margin of ±0.4%. That is not a gate too strict to see a small gain. It is a measurement that there is no gain, down to half a point. A looser gate would promote noise, and then the next round would be judged against a weaker "best".

Two more reasons not to chase small gains:
- A 1-point self-play gain is worth about 7 Elo. Rank 1 needs about 220. That road needs 30 promotions that each hold up, which the loop has not produced once since round 73.
- Self-play gains have not carried to live play at all. Round 73 beat round 66 in the gate and plays exactly the same against people, 59.0% against 59.2% over 350 games each.

**What I would keep and what I would change.** Keep the gate as it is; it is right for the job of not promoting noise. Judge experiments the way we now do, on pooled gates over several rounds, which sees half-point effects for free. Put the effort into changes that could give 3 points or more, which a single 4,000-game match sees clearly: the people-model data from today, the different training methods research is scoping, and the live-side clock work. If one of those moves the needle, the gate will pass it.
