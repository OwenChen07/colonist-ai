How human data is used today, and where it falls short.

**What goes in (every round, from about 4,500 captured games against people, 15% held out as the live yardstick):**
1. **Value targets:** the bot's own positions with the real result. Listed 12 times in the window since E37, roughly 10-15% of positions, but it is the same 3,800 games repeated.
2. **People's moves:** from the games the person won, as move targets only (E14, about 37k moves, 9% of move targets). No live effect was found.
3. **Starting positions:** 35% of self-play games start from a turn of a human game, so self-play sees people-made boards.
4. **A human opponent model:** a net trained to predict people's moves, playing one seat in 15% of self-play games (E13), so the bot practices against human-style play.

**The limitations:**
- **Hidden dev cards cut out the moves that matter most.** A capture is one-sided: we never see what cards the person holds. So their moves are kept only on turns where they hold no unplayed card. That drops most mid and late-game decisions, exactly where the knight race, Largest Army and timed card plays beat us. Both the move targets and the opponent model learn from a card-free slice, biased toward the early game. The opponent model in self-play is a patched variant that holds back card plays because of this.
- **The net only ever sees a count of the opponent's cards and never their hidden points.** That is true live and in self-play, so it is consistent, but it makes the late-game value a guess. Part of "too sure of a late lead" is a hidden point card the net cannot see.
- **Scale.** About 3,800 human games against 5,000-7,500 self-play games per round and a 12-18 round window. Repeating them raises their weight, not their information; E23 (more weight) made it no better.
- **Exact replay required.** A game must convert and replay move for move or it is dropped; about 5% are.
- **Value from the bot's seat only.** The person's positions get no value target, since their hand is unknown.

So any "learn from people" method inherits a card-free, early-weighted, small dataset unless we first infer the hidden cards, for example by backfilling them from when they are later played, which the replay could do for most games.
