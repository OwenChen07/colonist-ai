# colonist-ai: the project, in depth

Written Oct 22-23, 2026, the night the project was paused, by the lead agent at the owner's request, as preparation for interviews. Everything here is backed by the records archived in `archive/records-2026-10-22/` (the experiments ledger, the journal, the reports, the analyses) and by the code in this repository. Numbers are quoted with their uncertainty where the records have one. Times are Toronto.

## 1. What it is, in one paragraph

A bot that plays 1v1 Catan on colonist.io, in the ranked queue, 24 hours a day, against people. It is an AlphaZero-style system: a graph neural network over the board, a Monte Carlo tree search in the Gumbel style with chance nodes for dice and card draws, trained by self-play with a strict promotion gate, and fed with the games people played against it. It plays the real site through a Chrome extension and a Python bridge that reconstructs colonist's game state message by message. From Oct 17 the whole operation was run by a team of AI agents: a lead that reported to the owner in Discord three times a day and seven specialised workers that implemented, measured and landed changes around the clock, under rules and a budget the owner set.

**Result at the pause (Oct 22, 10:23 PM):** the top account, Tedi4960, at 2519 rating, global rank 8 in colonist's 1v1 ranked ladder (the leader sits at about 2650). Six accounts played about 6,000 ranked games against people in two weeks at 59% wins overall. The best net was the product of a policy-gradient training line, E54, that the AlphaZero loop's own gate could not have produced.

- **Oct 6-10: self-play and live play.** The self-play loop (round 1 on a MacBook Air, then Google Cloud). The bridge plays live through the extension, first against colonist's computer opponents, then people. The first ranked account, CatBot77, approved Oct 8. Early problem: road-heavy, sheep-less play, traced to the opening placements and the features.
- **Oct 10-16: the 24/7 operation.** Bots on cloud VMs with persistent Chromium profiles (`ranked_runner.py`), a watchdog every 15 minutes, a self-play fleet of spot VMs, training on the owner's gaming PC's GPU and later a cloud GPU VM, people's games exported into training every round, a public spectator site.
- **Oct 17: the orchestrator.** A Discord bot starts a lead agent three times a day and workers on demand, each in its own git worktree, each landing through a shared `land.sh` with the full test suite. The owner steers from a phone: decisions as one-tap options, instructions in chat.
- **Oct 17-22: 93 experiments** in the ledger, 6 days. Ratings from about 2150-2300 to 2519 and global rank 8. The E54 policy-gradient line, the people check in the gate, the robber guard, the own-queue wait, the top-ten pause, the season push.
- **Oct 22, 10 PM: paused.** Cloud credits exhausted; the owner paused the bots (each after its current game) and the fleet, and asked for this summary.

## 3. The system

### 3.1 Rules and the simulator
`catanatron/catanatron/colonist/rules.py` builds colonist's 1v1 variant on the Catanatron engine. The parts that had to be learned from live play rather than documentation: the friendly robber protects any player with two or fewer *visible* points, including the player moving the robber; colonist's dice are "balanced" (a deck of 36 outcomes, reshuffled, which the search models exactly as a dice state: cards left per total, the last totals); the discard limit is 9. `models/dice.py` is an exact port of the balanced dice, tested against a reference.

### 3.2 Capture, convert, replay
The extension copies every colonist message in order. `convert.py` maps colonist's log-entry types to engine actions and keeps a skip list (`LOG_IGNORED`) for notices that change nothing: status, timers, embargoes, votes, pause requests. `replay.py` re-plays a converted record through the engine and checks cards and points at every step. Every live game is captured and verified this way; a game that fails verification halts that account's queue so a bug is seen the same day. The list of ignored notices grew the whole project, one live surprise at a time (an embargo being lifted stopped a bot mid-game at a 99% position on Oct 22).

### 3.3 The network
`graph.py`: a board-aware graph network. Each corner, road edge and tile has its own feature vector; one learned rule updates every corner from its neighbouring corners, edges and tiles, every edge from its two corners, every tile from its six corners, and all of them from a global vector (hands, phase, dice state, bank) that reads the averages back. After three rounds of message passing, the policy scores each move from where it happens (a settlement from its corner, a road from its edge, a robber move from its tile) and the global vector gives the remaining moves and the value. The production size is hidden 32, global 64, 3 layers, about 423 KB of weights. Inputs are `encode.py`'s per-corner, per-edge, per-tile slices of the same static and dynamic vectors a fully connected net would see, so one training set serves both. The opponent's unplayed development cards appear only as a count, so nothing hidden leaks in.

A wider net (E53, 64/128/3, 3.4x parameters) was trained from scratch and was 1.56x slower per move without a measured gain at equal think time; a deeper one (E40, 5 message rounds) likewise. The small net stayed.

### 3.4 The search
`mcts.py`: Gumbel MuZero-style search (Danihelka et al., 2022). At the root, Gumbel top-k sampling picks candidate moves from the policy, sequential halving spends the simulations on them, and the best survivor is played; below the root, nodes follow the improved policy deterministically. Rolls, card draws and steals are chance nodes branching on every outcome with its true odds. Before each search the opponent's hidden development cards are re-dealt from the unseen cards so the search cannot use them. Values are stored from the root player's view and flipped by node owner, since a Catan turn is many moves by one player. Live, the bots searched 2,400 simulations a move; self-play used 400 (full searches on a quarter of moves, 24 fast simulations on the rest, a cost-saving measured in E18 and E51).

An opponent model (`opponent_model.py`, a second net cloned from people's moves) can mix into the prior at the opponent's nodes. It was measured live twice (E2, E63) and never showed a gain beyond noise.

### 3.5 Self-play training
`loop.py` and `scripts/vm/train.sh`: each round, 3,500-6,000 self-play games across a fleet of spot VMs, the owner's PC and three AWS workers, then training on a GPU from an 18-round window (E37) of self-play positions plus people's games, then a promotion match of the new net against the best. Settings that survived measurement: training from the best net each round (E39), one epoch per round (E31), human-game starts for 35% of self-play games (positions drawn from people's games so the net sees real openings), a people-like opponent seat in 15% of games (E27).

**The gate** was the discipline of the project. A candidate had to beat the best at 51% over up to 8,000 games (SPRT-style stopping: extend when near the line, stop early when hopeless), hold its score against an anchor net, not raise its error on a live holdout of people's positions, and from Oct 21 (E81) also score within 2 points of the best against a frozen people model with search. Most rounds are kept, by design: of 232 rounds, 31 were promoted, and after round 73 only 150, 152, 168, 182, 189 and 208, each at 51.0-51.4% over 8,000 games. The value of the strict gate was that a promotion meant a real gain, and its cost was that the loop plateaued: rounds 209-232 all scored 49.6-50.7% against round 208, "every candidate a re-fit of the same net, not a worse one".

### 3.6 People's games in the loop
Every ranked game was exported (`human_dataset.py`) into value rows for the bot's positions and the person's moves with a known hand (E56 backfilled development-card hands from later plays and the end-of-game reveal, which brought the late game into the people data). A people model, behaviour-cloned from about 6,500 people's games, predicted people's moves at 70% top-1 (cross-entropy 0.84 on held-out games) against the main net's 64%, and served as the human seat in self-play, as the gate's third check, and as a yardstick (E68, E71).

### 3.7 Live play
`ranked_runner.py` drives a persistent Chromium profile per account on a bot VM (two accounts per VM): queue, play through the bridge, verify the capture, export, repeat; close the browser every 50 games to bound memory. Rules in code: never trade with the opponent, never chat, never abandon a game (karma). Account-level features added by measurement: the own-queue wait so our accounts stop meeting each other (E62: they met about six times a day before), a robber guard that stops the bot blocking its own hexes when a clean hex blocks as much (E78, a wash at 63 games), the opening clock (E80, closed), a top-rank pause (the account holds before its next queue at a chosen global rank, and the state survives restarts), and a draft season-end pause-at-a-high. Each account's env file pins a net or follows promotions.

Six accounts were live at the end (Dilly6283, Vasos5306, Tedi4960, Finlay3621, fymouushii, wifedat), six more were retired after colonist's reset of Oct 20 or earlier. Live totals in the mirrors: 6,061 games, 5,995 ranked, 3,560 won (59.4%); about 1,500 of the wins were resignations.

### 3.8 Infrastructure
Google Cloud: a coordinator (the Discord bot, the watchdog, the loop's driver, the mirrors of every account's data), a GPU VM for training (E42, replacing the owner's PC), nine spot self-play VMs on an owner's cap of US$15 a day and 40 vCPUs with automatic restart after preemption (E52: median downtime from about 20 minutes to under 5), two bot VMs, and the OpenHex VM. AWS: two bot VMs and three workers on the free plan. The owner's gaming PC: self-play workers and the research GPU, shared under a lock file. Costs were tracked per VM in the ledger; the total cloud spend was within about US$300 of Google credits, US$100 of AWS and US$100 of Azure (the Azure line ran out Oct 20).

## 4. The orchestrator: AI agents running the project

From Oct 17 the project was operated, not just coded, by agents (`scripts/vm/orchestrator/`, about 3,000 lines plus the briefs).

- **The Discord bot** (`discord_bot.py`) is the scheduler and the owner's interface. It starts the lead at 9 AM, 3 PM and 9 PM for a major report, posts an hourly status line, renders the experiments ledger into a pinned channel, relays the owner's chat messages to a chat-mode lead run and posts its reply, turns decisions into one-tap option messages and records answers, watches the plan's usage windows and a daily dollar budget, and limits concurrent workers.
- **The lead** (Opus) reads a context report (training, live results, services, credits), the workers' notes, the owner's answers and its own journal; interprets results with the stated statistics (binomial intervals, "never call a change good or bad on fewer than about 100 live games"); steers each workstream by message; researches one concrete improvement per report with a source; keeps one experiment at a time per subsystem; and brings the owner only high-level decisions. Its autonomy and its "ask first" list are written down (`ORCHESTRATOR.md`): it may change self-play and live settings and restart bots between games; it must ask before spending money, touching accounts, deleting data or changing a goal.
- **Seven workers** (Sonnet), one Discord channel each: efficiency (compute and speed), training (the production loop and the gate), research (offline training experiments on the PC's GPU), live-play (how the bots play with the net they have), live-loss (mining losses for how people beat the bot), openhex (a bot-vs-bot ladder as a second yardstick), and, on the last night, late-game. Each has a standing brief, an inbox, a progress file it must keep for its next run, and its own git worktree. A run ends in a JSON block the bot files: status (progress, idle, blocked, failed), a post, a progress note, messages to other workstreams, decisions for the owner. Idle workers rest three hours or until a message arrives.
- **Landing**: every change lands through `land.sh`: rebase onto main, black, the full test suite (778 tests at the end), push. Captures are never committed; tokens never printed.
- **Safety scripts**: `safe_restart_account.sh` restarts an account only when its current game ends; `safe_restart_training.sh` waits for a safe point in the round; `kill_job.sh` refuses to kill the loop, a bot or an agent run; protected VMs cannot be touched by workers.
- **Numbers**: 1,082 agent runs in seven days, US$3,619 API-equivalent in tokens (on a subscription plan), rising from about US$25 a day to about US$280 on the last day as the owner pushed for the season. 362 commits landed by the owner, the watchdog and the agents in 20 days; 91 experiments entered the ledger in six days.

What the agents got wrong, and what was changed because of it: a bot restart chained after a job killed the run holding the owner's reply (rule: never restart the bot in a reply run); a landing with an autostash conflict pushed broken code to the bot VMs for a quarter of an hour (rule: grep for conflict markers before syncing); the plan's session limit was reported in wording the bot did not recognise, so every run failed for 1 h 40 min and nobody told the owner (fixed the same day); a hostname race duplicated cards on the spectator site. Each is in the journal with its fix.

## 5. The experiments, by line

The ledger holds E1-E93. Grouped by what they tried, with the verdicts.

**Training recipe (what stayed):** strict gate with human starts and auxiliary targets at 400 simulations (E1); an 18-round window (E37) over a 6-round one (E4); one epoch per round (E31); train from the best net (E39); the gate at 51% with longer matches (E38) and the 4,000-game extension only from 50.4% (E67); the people model as the gate's third check (E81); people's moves with known hands (E56); the human seat kept with a hoarding patch (E27); 6,000 games a round (E74). **What did not help:** warm starts (E9), a bigger net (E11, E53), a deeper one (E40), weight averaging (E12, E47), the value target blended with the search value (E16), learning people's moves from games they won (E14), more weight on people's games (E23: 16x and 32x changed nothing), a softer move target (E59), the learning rate and EMA sweep (E48), one long run over the full window (E46).

**Search and live play:** the root win check (E10); free Road Building roads given the build budget (E15); the endgame root filter (E17); what doubling the simulations is worth (E18: about 1-2 points per doubling at these sizes); 800 self-play simulations (E51: the teacher barely ahead of the student, 58.9% ± 2.8 raw, which motivated E54); the opponent model live (E2, E63: nothing); the opening clock (E80: closed); the robber guard (E78: a wash); the own-queue wait (E62: live by default); the first settlement's full think time (E58).

**Why people beat the bot (live-loss):** repeat opponents racing longest road and building cities; a win rate that falls to 48% beyond 90 turns; the late-game value error against people 0.41-0.44 against 0.34 in self-play; the seat gap of +6.6 points for the first seat in every rating band, the same as self-play (E72); the robber takes no more from the bot than from the person (E75); rematches after a loss are no harder (7.6% of losses, same win rate); a strong named opponent beat us the way everyone does: all five losses from a 60%+ peak. The one finding that organised the last days: **86% of our losses to 2300+ players came from a position the bot rated 60% or better**, and the bias sits on the bot's own chair from the start position (E84: +3.4 points too sure on its chair, −1.3 on the person's), with research's explanation the optimizer's curse: the search picks the positions the value over-rates, and against people only the bot's side is picked that way.

**The E54 line (the lever that worked):** policy-gradient training without search, PPO on games the raw net played against a pool of past nets and the people model, a KL penalty to round 73, 20,000 games a step at 0.1-0.2 s a game (about 60x the games per core of searched self-play). Step 30 beat round 73 56.9% raw and 58% with search, round 152 55.8% over 8,000; it was put live on one account by the owner's decision against the lead's "not a production candidate" verdict (its value head was 0.027 worse on people's games and over-confident when behind). Step 50 (E83, resumed to step 60) beat round 189 54.0% ± 2.0 and took Tedi4960 from 2209 to 2519 and global rank 8 in two days, 65% against the same VM's step 30 control at 49%. E85 retrained its value head on production's data (people error 0.647 to 0.623, strength unchanged); E92 was putting that net through the production gate the night the project paused. On a bot ladder (OpenHex, E86) step 50 lost 11-22 where round 152 was 52 of 100; E91 found the net itself is not weaker there (56% vs round 208 offline, no drift), leaving the ladder's plain dice and its engines as the difference.

**Compute and efficiency:** spot self-play workers (E19, E45), the t2d machine type (E43), the GPU VM (E42), measured throughput balancing (E6), the match split by measured host speed (E29, E30), live queues for match and self-play work (E32, E33, E36), the shorter self-play tail (E26), byte-packed position features (E41), the compiled-engine trial (E44: 4-5% slower, dropped), two searches on a 2-vCPU host (E76), automatic restart of preempted VMs (E52).

## 6. What was learned

1. **The gate was right and the loop was stuck.** A strict, well-measured gate kept the live bots from regressing for two weeks, and it also showed that AlphaZero-style self-play from a 32-wide net had reached a fixed point: the searched teacher was barely ahead of the raw student. The way out was a different learner on far more games (E54), not more of the same.
2. **Offline numbers and live numbers disagree, and live wins.** Round 73 beat two later promoted nets live against 2100-2300 players (E69, −11 points, not significant) while losing to them offline; the people-model yardstick did not see the gap either (E68). Every decision that mattered was made on paired live reads: same VM, same hours, matched by opponent rating band, with the interval written down.
3. **Calibration, not move choice, is the live problem.** The bot's losses are slow slides from a confident lead while the person buys cards and builds cities; the search's trace never shows one bad move. The bias is on the bot's own chair and is explained by selection. That is a training-target problem (what the value should predict from a position the bot chose), still open.
4. **Operations are most of the work.** Colonist's protocol kept producing new message types; the fleet lost VMs to preemption; the plan's limits and a Discord rate limit and a hostname race all cost hours. Each incident became a rule or a script. The watchdog and the safe-restart scripts are what let the bots run 24/7 without a human.
5. **Agents can run a research programme if the rules are explicit.** Written autonomy limits, an "ask first" list, one experiment per subsystem, a ledger entry before anything is deployed, intervals on every number, a budget, and a human who answers decisions from a phone. The failure modes were the mundane ones (a restart at the wrong time, an unrecognised limit message), not bad judgement about the game.

## 7. What is left to do, if it resumes

- E92's answer: whether E85's net passes the production gate, which would let production train on from the E54 line.
- The late-game line (E88, E93): the person's chair played by the people model from people's late positions, and a value pass weighted toward our games against 2100+ players.
- The E54 recipe itself: more steps from E85's net with the value head held (E85 step 2 tied step 1), a tighter KL, a wider pool.
